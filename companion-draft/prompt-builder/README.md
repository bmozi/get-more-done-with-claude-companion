# When you do not know what to ask

Use Chapters 1 and 5. Chapter 43 develops testing for prompts you will reuse; Chapter 44 develops chaining, which you also use when a project passes one step's output to the next. This exercise needs only a text conversation and saved notes. The example is designed teaching material, not a record of an actual Claude or human session.

## Begin in ordinary words

Copy the starting request, adding a harmless situation of your own:

> I am not sure how to ask for what I need. Here is my situation: [describe it in ordinary words]. Help me figure out what to ask. Ask one question at a time, then draft a simple prompt using my answers. Leave missing information visible and let me check the prompt before you carry out the task.

Answer a question only from what you know. Ask for an explanation or options if you do not understand it. Once the important facts are clear, ask for the prompt. Review it, correct it, then say whether to proceed. Keep both the proposed prompt and your checked version.

## Try a learning task

Use this fictional situation: “I want to learn enough about spreadsheets to organize a small list, but I do not know what to ask.” The learner has twenty minutes, can enter text and numbers in cells, and wants to total quantities for a hobby supply box. The spreadsheet application is unspecified. Use invented entries: pencils, three packs at $4 each; markers, two packs at $6 each. The supplied prices are per pack. No real purchases or financial records are involved.

Give the assistant the rough opening first. Answer its questions from the supplied facts; if it asks which application, say it is not decided. Ask it to prepare a short tutoring prompt and wait. A useful prompt should ask for a small explanation, an attempt by the learner and a changed example, rather than just creating a finished sheet. It should leave application-specific instructions unresolved or use a generic table.

Before carrying out the proposed prompt, look for invented software, extra study time, or a different learning goal. Correct those details. Ask the tutor to explain why quantity times price per pack gives the line cost. Make the calculation yourself: the two line costs are $12 and $12, totaling $24. Having the assistant produce a correct table does not show that you can explain it.

## Change one fact

The learner now has only ten minutes and wants just the pencil line. Revise the prompt before proceeding. A two-item, twenty-minute lesson no longer matches the request. Then change the pencil price to $5 per pack: three packs cost $15. Explain the unit relationship without the earlier answer visible.

## Keep the method small

Save the rough opening, questions and answers, first prompt, your corrections, final request and actual output if run. Note an unresolved fact and a question you learned to ask. Do not judge success by how long or impressive the generated prompt sounds. It should preserve your intention and help you recognize a useful result.
